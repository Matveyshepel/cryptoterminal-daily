Bitcoin's Dry-Powder Question: What Binance's Shrinking Stablecoin Pile Does and Does Not Tell Us

November 10, 2026 · CryptoTerminal Daily

Every market bounce needs buyers, and in crypto a large share of that buying power sits in stablecoins parked on exchanges. That is why a fresh reading on Binance deserves attention. According to Novaque Research data published through CryptoQuant and cited by CryptoSlate, the exchange's tracked ERC-20 stablecoin reserves stood near $32 billion on Nov. 10, down from roughly $41 billion to $42 billion in late October.

The timing is hard to ignore. The contraction follows the Oct. 29 hack at Binance and the later reopening of withdrawals. A separate analysis by community analyst oinonen_t, also published through CryptoQuant, measured a 25.6% decline in Binance's ERC-20 stablecoin reserves from an early-October starting point. The same work found a much smaller 7.6% drop in the exchange's bitcoin reserves, to approximately 673,000 BTC. Read side by side, the figures point to a bigger proportional retreat in dollars than in coins.

What the number measures

It would be easy to turn this into a simple story about money leaving the largest exchange. The sources do not support that leap. The balances cover ERC-20 tokens only, which means stablecoins held on other networks are not counted. Novaque itself cautions that wallet restructuring and cross-chain transfers may explain part of the contraction. The two analyses also use different starting points, one early October and one late October, and their exact wallet coverage is not specified. Combining them would overstate what is actually known about the effect of the hack.

There is a second, more basic limit. CryptoQuant describes stablecoin reserves as potential buying power. Potential is the key word. An inventory figure says how many dollars sit in exchange addresses; it does not say how many of those dollars are bidding for bitcoin at any given price.

What the order book showed

To test that, CryptoSlate compared live order books. Around 10:42 UTC on Nov. 10, Binance's public BTC-USDT book showed approximately 1.98 million USDT of displayed buy orders within 5 basis points, or 0.05%, below its midprice. A near-simultaneous snapshot of OKX showed about 0.98 million USDT inside the same window. On that narrow measure, Binance still displayed more nearby buying interest than its rival.

The caveat is just as important as the finding. Orders can change before they execute, and a single pair of snapshots cannot establish lasting depth, a decline from earlier periods or a return to pre-hack conditions. The same Novaque comparison put OKX's tracked stablecoins near $9 billion and trending lower, so there is no sign that Binance's lost reserves simply reappeared at OKX.

The macro backdrop

Liquidity on one exchange is only half of the picture. The other half is the cost of money. Binance Research reported on Tuesday that eight of the 10 central banks it tracks raised rates over the past six months, including Sweden's increase announced on Nov. 4. It also said the momentum of tightening has eased since its July peak. In its view, whether that easing continues into early 2027 will be key to judging whether the backdrop is turning less hostile for crypto.

The firm pointed to the last cycle as a guide. Bitcoin bottomed in November 2022, and its six-month return turned positive in February 2023, before any of the central banks in its index began cutting. Its conclusion was that bitcoin has tended to move with the direction of global policy rather than its level.

In the near term, the direction in the United States is still toward tighter money. Fed governor Christopher Waller said he was leaning toward a quarter-point hike in December, and the odds of a 25-basis-point move rose to 96.3% on Tuesday, according to CME Group's FedWatch tool. Bitcoin traded around $74,000 on Nov. 10, and Cointelegraph noted it was 41% below its all-time high, with the price down 30% over one year.

Our take

The shrinking stablecoin balance is a warning light, not a verdict. It tells us that the most visible pool of dollars on the largest exchange is smaller than it was a few weeks ago, and that some of the change may be bookkeeping across networks rather than money walking out the door. What it cannot tell us is how much of that pool will actually be spent on bitcoin.

For now, the displayed order books suggest buyers have not disappeared, while the policy path suggests they have little reason to rush. Until exchange stablecoin balances rebuild, any recovery from current levels will depend less on Binance's war chest and more on fresh dollars arriving from outside the market. That is the number worth watching in the weeks ahead.
